  <h1>Bias Is a Four-Letter Word</h1>

  <p>Imagine this...</p>

  <ul>
    <li>You are Black</li>
    <li>You are a woman</li>
    <li>You are an immigrant from a stigmatized country</li>
    <li>You also have 20 years of teaching experience </li>
  </ul>

<ol>
    <li>
      Students burst into laughter and exchange glances with one another,
      when you are trying to explain something important (in all seriousness) to the class.
    </li>

  <li>
    A student aggressively shoves their completed exam into your open hand.
    Their answer to your smiling face and “are you finished?” is only “Here!”.
  </li>

  <li>
    A pair of students comes to your office to take issue with incorrect responses
    on their exam. Being disappointed with the outcome, one abruptly walks away declaring
    that she is “leaving before ...(doing something)... because you are making her angry”.
  </li>

  <li>
    You, in an effort to seem relatable, tell a student who is wearing an Arsenal
    Football Club jersey that your family follows Arsenal. They ask to give you a high-five.
    You accept; but they hit you so hard that your hand stings for a prolonged period of time.
    It is clear that they intentionally hurt you.
  </li>

  <li>
    You enjoy teaching and devote much time to course preparation. You take care that
    your syllabus, LMS course site, assessments, and rubrics are well presented and thoughtfully
    aligned with course learning objectives. You’ve been teaching at the college level for over
    20 years. Yet, semester after semester, your course evaluations include comments like:
    “Horrible, horrible, horrible” and “Do not teach this class again”.
  </li>

  <li>
    After taking his final exam, a white male student twice your size stands extremely
    close with his back turned to the class. Looking down at you, he begins complaining about
    his grade and demands extra credit. You step aside—to escape from him, to restore your
    personal space, and to continue observing the class you are proctoring. He persists,
    defiantly standing closer than before and again obstructing your view, until other students
    approach the two of you to return their exams. You wonder if he might be waiting for you
    outside the classroom and could hurt you.
  </li>
</ol>

  <p>
    Now that you have imagined these things, what would you say
    are the chances that anonymous student evaluations of teaching (SETs) for minoritized
    faculty are sometimes biased, harassing, or intimidating? 
  </p>

  <p>
    Twice a year, course evaluations containing unfounded, fabricated, and hurtful remarks push me into a state of despair. Feeling helpless and hopeless, I wonder “what I have done to deserve this” as I await the inevitable reprimand from people with power to hire/fire.
  </p>

  <p>
    This year, I am fighting back the one way I know how: with DATA. Using a combination of Claude.AI and ChatGPT I compiled resources and designed a
    <a href="https://github.com/sallybseraphin/Teaching_While_XYZ/blob/31d7890191ada55d126157af71ca53180300b9b7/Instructions%20for%20Using%20AI%20to%20Assess%20Bias%20in%20Student%20Course%20Evaluations.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      <strong>SET Bias Assessment Tool</strong>
    </a>
    to help other weary educators highlight evidence of discrimination in their course evaluations.
    As information, student feedback can be helpful. The course evaluation should not be used as a weapon. When it
    harms, let’s call it what it is:
    <a href="https://github.com/sallybseraphin/Teaching_While_XYZ/blob/31d7890191ada55d126157af71ca53180300b9b7/SET_Bias_Category_Summary_Table.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      <strong>BIAS, a four-letter word.</strong>
    </a>
  </p>
