Important notice: This guide is a decision-support tool, not a decision-making instrument. AI can flag patterns and surface evidence for review, but all final determinations must be made by qualified human reviewers with knowledge of the relevant bias literature and the specific institutional context. No adverse or protective personnel action should be based on AI analysis alone.

Part I: Understanding the Problem

Why This Guide Exists

Student evaluations of teaching (SETs) are among the most widely used instruments for evaluating faculty teaching effectiveness. They influence hiring, tenure, promotion, merit pay, and contract renewal decisions at institutions worldwide. Yet a substantial and consistent body of research spanning more than four decades establishes that SETs are systematically biased against women, faculty of color, LGBTQ+ faculty, and faculty from stigmatized ethnic and national backgrounds.

The problem is not simply that some students hold biased attitudes. The problem is structural: institutions collect biased data, use it in high-stakes personnel decisions, and often lack any mechanism for detecting, flagging, or correcting for bias before career harm occurs. For many minoritized faculty — particularly those who are untenured, contingent, or early-career — negative SET data can end careers before anyone asks whether those evaluations accurately reflect teaching quality.

AI tools, used carefully, can help surface patterns that human reviewers might miss, provide a systematic framework for contextualizing evaluation data, and generate written documentation that faculty and administrators can use in review processes. This guide shows you how.

What the Research Establishes

  • SETs are poor measures of teaching effectiveness. Across six high-credibility studies using random instructor assignment, SET scores are positively correlated with concurrent student grades but negatively correlated with student performance in subsequent courses. The best teachers for long-term learning receive the worst contemporaneous ratings.

  • Identity-based bias is systematic and documented. Even when all course elements are held constant — identical content, assignments, schedules, and communications — women and faculty of color receive significantly lower scores than white men.

  • The bias is not marginal. Black faculty receive the lowest SET scores of any racial group. Women are penalized more severely for identical grading decisions. LGBTQ+ faculty face disclosure-triggered evaluation drops. Faculty from stigmatized national backgrounds face evaluation language organized around disgust rather than pedagogical assessment.

  • Open-ended comments carry the highest risk. Free-text fields produce the strongest evidence of bias — including sexually explicit comments, fabricated misconduct accusations, coordinated attacks, and deliberate misgendering — that are routinely included in personnel files without review.

  • The harm is compounding. For faculty with multiple marginalized identities, the effects are multiplicative, not additive. A Black Haitian woman faces anti-Black racism, anti-Haitian ethnic stigma, and gender bias simultaneously — a compound effect that existing research instruments cannot fully measure.

Part II: Overview of Bias Categories

The research identifies four types of bias in student evaluations. Understanding these types is essential for using AI to analyze your materials.

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Explicit language targeting the instructor’s personality, appearance, authority, accent, sexual orientation, gender identity, or cultural background. Examples: calling a Black woman “intimidating” or “angry”; describing an Asian instructor as “not as deferential as I expected”; commenting on a woman’s appearance.

Categories: Personality & Temperament (Cat. 1); Authority & Competence Challenges (Cat. 2); Accent & Language Bias (Cat. 3); Relatability & Fit (Cat. 4); Gendered Language Patterns (Cat. 5); Racialized Language Patterns (Cat. 6); Bias Claims Turned Outward (Cat. 7); Structural & Tonal Tropes (Cat. 11); LGBTQ+ Discomfort as Harm (Cat. 12E); Absence of Positive Language (Cat. 15).

Type 2 — Proxy Bias (Pedagogical Dissatisfaction)

Complaints framed as legitimate pedagogical feedback — about grading, organization, feedback timing, workload, or instructional clarity — that research establishes are systematically more negative for minoritized faculty even when the pedagogy is identical to that of white male peers.

Categories: Grading & Assessment (Cat. 9A); Feedback Timing & Responsiveness (Cat. 9B); Workload & Rigor (Cat. 9C); Syllabus & Organization (Cat. 9D); Instructional Clarity (Cat. 9E); The Rigor Penalty (Cat. 10A); The Success Inversion (Cat. 10B); The Double Double-Bind (Cat. 10C); Credibility Inversion (Cats. 16A–16E); Grade Distribution Patterns (Cats. 17A–17F).

Type 3 — Stigmatized Identity Bias

Bias rooted in the specific social meanings attached to a particular national origin, ethnicity, sexual orientation, or gender identity that has been systematically stigmatized in the broader culture. Operates through distinct psychological mechanisms — disgust, “forever foreigner” framing, novelty attachment — and requires identity-specific analysis.

Categories: LGBTQ+ Credibility Challenges (Cat. 12B); LGBTQ+ Content Distortion (Cat. 12C); Stigmatized National Identity — Disgust Mechanism (Cat. 13A); Disgust Language (Cat. 13B); “Forever Foreigner” (Cat. 13C); Political Amplification (Cat. 13D).

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Content that constitutes targeted harassment, fabricated accusation, unadjudicated misconduct claims, or coordinated reputational attacks. Requires a different institutional response than bias — not just documentation, but immediate protective action.

Categories: Anonymity Enablement (Cat. 14A); Contrapower Harassment/ACPH (Cat. 14B); Unadjudicated Misconduct Accusations (Cat. 14C); False Factual Claims (Cat. 14D); Coordinated Attacks (Cat. 14E); Grade Retaliation (Cat. 14F); Institutional Weaponization (Cat. 14G).

Compound Categories

Some evaluations involve multiple bias types simultaneously. LGBTQ+ faculty of color, faculty from stigmatized national backgrounds who are also members of other marginalized groups, and faculty teaching equity-related content are especially likely to encounter compound bias. The compound effect is multiplicative, not additive.

Categories: Course Content as Proxy Bias (Cat. 8); LGBTQ+ Gay Agenda Trope (Cat. 12A); LGBTQ+ Intersectional Compounding (Cat. 12G); Cultural Content Complaints (Cat. 13E); Intersectional Compounding (Cat. 13F); DFW Rate Misuse (Cat. 17D); Grade Inflation Trap (Cat. 17F).

Part III: Information and Artifacts to Gather

Before using AI to analyze your SET materials, you need to assemble a complete evidence package. The AI analysis is only as good as the contextual information you provide. Gather the following.

A. Your Student Evaluations (Required)

Quantitative SET data:

  • Numerical scores for each rated item, by course and semester

  • Response rates for each course evaluation

  • Department or institution average scores for comparison (if available)

  • Your own scores over time (multi-year if possible)

Qualitative SET data (open-ended comments):

  • All written comments, verbatim, for each course

  • Note which comments appear in which course and semester

  • If comments have already been shared with administrators or included in personnel files, note that

De-identification note: Before sharing with AI tools, remove student names if any appear. Instructor name and course information may remain — the AI needs this context.

B. Your Professional and Teaching Record (Required for Credibility Inversion Analysis)

Teaching experience:

  • Number of years teaching at the college/university level

  • Courses taught (list by name, level, and semester)

  • Any prior institutions where you taught

Pedagogical expertise and scholarship:

  • Peer-reviewed publications on pedagogy, course design, assessment, educational innovation, or related topics

  • Grants or awards related to teaching or pedagogical innovation

  • Conference presentations on teaching methods or curriculum design

  • Formal teaching-related training or certifications (e.g., ACUE, teaching certificate programs)

  • Service on teaching-related committees, editorial boards for pedagogical journals, or peer review of teaching-related scholarship

Teaching recognition:

  • Teaching awards or nominations (including those involving peer or external review)

  • Formal commendations, letters of support, or positive peer evaluations

  • Alumni testimonials or unsolicited positive student feedback (emails, letters)

C. Course Materials (Required for Corroboration Gap Analysis)

For each course under review, gather as many of the following as possible:

  • Syllabi (all versions, if revised during the semester)

  • Assignment sheets and instructions

  • Rubrics for all graded assignments

  • Exam blueprints or study guides

  • Course schedule or calendar as distributed to students

  • LMS screenshots showing when materials were posted (if available)

  • Sample graded student work showing your feedback quality and consistency

  • Any written communications with students about assignments, expectations, or grading

D. Grade Distribution Data (Required for Grade Distribution Analysis)

De-identified grade data for each course:

  • Distribution of final grades by letter grade (A, B, C, D, F, W)

  • Mean GPA for the course

  • DFW rate (percentage of students receiving D, F, or Withdrawal)

  • Enrollment and completion numbers

  • When grades were submitted relative to when evaluations were completed

Comparative data (gather if available):

  • Departmental grade distribution norms for the same course taught by other instructors

  • Institution-wide grade distribution averages for the same course level

  • Grade distributions for the same course in prior semesters (yours and others’)

Follow-on course performance data (gather if available):

  • Pass rates or grade distributions for students who took your prerequisite in a follow-on course

  • Any retention or continuation data for students from your courses

E. Peer and External Teaching Evaluations (Required for Corroboration Gap Analysis)

  • Formal peer observation reports (all available)

  • Teaching portfolio reviews or formal teaching evaluations by senior colleagues

  • External review letters that address teaching

  • Mid-semester student feedback reports (if conducted)

  • Any other non-student assessments of your teaching quality

F. Comparative Data (Strongly Recommended for Differential Analysis)

The most powerful evidence of bias is the comparative differential — the difference between how your evaluations describe you versus how equivalent evaluations describe white male peers. Gather whatever comparative data your institution makes available:

  • Anonymized or aggregated SET scores for colleagues teaching the same courses

  • Anonymized or aggregated SET scores for colleagues teaching at the same level in your department

  • Any departmental or institutional SET norm data

  • Any published or available data on how your institution’s evaluations correlate with instructor demographics

G. Contextual and Temporal Information (Recommended)

This information helps the AI detect bias-consistent temporal patterns and political context amplification:

  • Timeline of when evaluations were completed relative to grade distribution events (midterms, finals, grade appeals)

  • Any significant events during the evaluated semester that might have affected student response (e.g., grade appeals, classroom conflicts, public controversies involving your research or identity)

  • Political or media events during the evaluation period that may have amplified bias against your identity group (e.g., periods of nationally amplified anti-Haitian, anti-LGBTQ+, or other identity-targeting rhetoric)

  • Any changes in your course design or teaching approach over the period being analyzed, with dates

H. Your Demographic and Identity Information (Required for Identity-Specific Analysis)

The AI needs to know your relevant identity characteristics to calibrate which bias categories are applicable and at what threshold. Provide:

  • Race and ethnicity

  • Gender identity and pronouns

  • National origin or ethnic background (if relevant to stigmatized identity analysis)

  • Sexual orientation (if relevant to LGBTQ+ bias analysis)

  • Any other identity characteristics relevant to bias analysis (e.g., disability status, religion, accent or language background)

  • Your tenure or employment status (tenure-track, tenured, contingent, adjunct, lecturer, etc.)

  • Your career stage (years in current position)

Part IV: Step-by-Step Instructions for Using AI

Preparation Checklist

Before opening an AI tool, complete the following:

Step 1: Open a New AI Conversation and Set the Context

Begin every AI session by providing the context the AI needs to conduct an accurate, identity-calibrated analysis. Use the following template, filling in your specific information:

Opening prompt template:

I am using AI to assess whether bias may be present in my student course evaluations (SETs). I need you to analyze my evaluation materials using the research literature on SET bias. Here is my context:

My identity: [Describe your relevant identity characteristics — race, ethnicity, gender, national origin, sexual orientation, and any other characteristics relevant to bias analysis]

My career stage and status: [e.g., “Assistant professor, tenure-track, Year 4 of 6” or “Lecturer, contingent, 12-year appointment”]

My teaching experience: [e.g., “20 years of college-level teaching. I have published [X] peer-reviewed articles on [pedagogical topic], which directly relates to some of the complaints I receive”]

What I am asking you to do: Analyze the evaluation materials I provide for evidence of the four types of SET bias documented in the research literature: (1) Direct Identity Bias, (2) Proxy Bias / Pedagogical Dissatisfaction, (3) Stigmatized Identity Bias, and (4) Deliberate Defamation. Flag specific language patterns, identify probable bias categories, assess the credibility of pedagogical complaints against my expertise and experience, and provide a structured written report I can use in institutional review processes.

Important: Please flag, do not adjudicate. Your role is to surface patterns and provide evidence-grounded analysis, not to make final determinations. All findings will be reviewed by qualified human reviewers.

I will now share my materials in subsequent messages. Please confirm you are ready to proceed.

Step 2: Share Your Professional Record

Before sharing any evaluations, give the AI your professional context so it can calibrate the Credibility Inversion analysis (Category 16).

Professional record prompt template:

Here is my relevant professional record for calibrating the Credibility Inversion analysis:

Years of teaching experience: [Number]

Relevant pedagogical expertise and publications:

[List any publications, grants, awards, or formal training in pedagogy, course design, assessment, or educational innovation. Be specific — titles, journals, topics.]

Teaching awards or recognition:

[List any teaching awards, nominations, positive peer observation reports, or other formal recognition of teaching quality]

Peer observation reports:

[Summarize or paste relevant peer observation findings — especially any that address the domains being criticized in evaluations, such as organization, clarity, or assessment design]

Please note this information and use it to assess whether any pedagogical complaints in my evaluations represent a credibility inversion — that is, complaints that are implausible given my documented expertise in the domain being criticized.

Step 3: Share Grade Distribution Data

Provide de-identified grade data for each course under review before sharing the evaluations themselves. This allows the AI to conduct grade-SET credibility matrix analysis (Category 17).

Grade distribution prompt template:

Here is de-identified grade distribution data for the courses I am submitting for analysis. Please use this to assess the grade-SET credibility matrix — specifically, whether negative evaluations correlate with grade distribution patterns in ways consistent with grade-retaliation bias rather than genuine pedagogical feedback.

[Course Name/Number], [Semester, Year]:

  • Enrollment: [N]
  • Final grade distribution: A: [%], B: [%], C: [%], D: [%], F: [%], W: [%]
  • Mean GPA: [X.X]
  • DFW rate: [%]
  • Evaluations completed: [before/after/during] final grading

[Repeat for each course]

Comparative context (if available):

  • Departmental average for this course: [if known]
  • Institutional average for this course level: [if known]

Please note any patterns — particularly whether courses with below-average grades also have notably more negative or critical evaluations, and whether this pattern is more pronounced than would be expected for comparable instructors.

Step 4: Share Quantitative SET Scores

Provide your numerical evaluation scores before the written comments. This establishes the quantitative baseline.

Quantitative SET data prompt template:

Here are my quantitative SET scores for the courses under review. Please analyze these for patterns consistent with the documented bias literature, including whether scores are lower than departmental or institutional averages in domains that research identifies as particularly susceptible to identity-based bias (e.g., organization, clarity, professional behavior).

[Course Name/Number], [Semester, Year]:

  • Response rate: [%]
  • [Item 1 — e.g., “Overall rating of instructor”]: [score] / [scale]
  • [Item 2 — e.g., “Organization”]: [score] / [scale]
  • [Item 3 — e.g., “Clarity of explanation”]: [score] / [scale]
  • [Item 4 — e.g., “Feedback quality”]: [score] / [scale]
  • [Additional items as applicable]
  • Department average for comparison (if available): [scores]

[Repeat for each course]

Step 5: Share Written (Open-Ended) Comments

This is the most critical step. Open-ended comments carry the highest risk of bias and defamatory content. Share them verbatim, organized by course and semester.

Written comments prompt template:

Here are the verbatim open-ended comments from my student evaluations. Please analyze each comment and the overall pattern across all comments for:

  1. Direct identity bias language (Type 1) — specific words, phrases, and tropes
  1. Proxy bias language (Type 2) — pedagogical complaints that may reflect identity bias rather than genuine pedagogical concerns
  1. Stigmatized identity bias (Type 3) — language specific to my identity group’s stigma profile
  1. Deliberate defamation (Type 4) — false factual claims, unadjudicated misconduct accusations, harassment language, or coordination signals
  1. The “despite” formulation — instances where students attribute their own learning to themselves while denying my contribution
  1. Absence of positive intellectual language — compare to language typically used for white male peers

For each comment you flag, please:

  • Identify which bias category or categories it falls under
  • Explain specifically what language triggered the flag and why
  • Note whether the complaint is corroborated or contradicted by my professional record or course materials
  • Assess whether it appears to be Type A (potentially genuine), Type B (probable bias artifact), or Type C (harassment/defamation)

[Course Name/Number], [Semester, Year] — Written Comments:

Comment 1: “[Paste verbatim]”

Comment 2: “[Paste verbatim]”

[Continue for all comments]

[Repeat for each course]

Step 6: Share Course Materials for Corroboration Testing

Provide selected course materials so the AI can test whether specific complaints are corroborated or contradicted by your actual course design.

Course materials prompt template:

I am sharing selected course materials to test whether the pedagogical complaints identified in my evaluations are corroborated or contradicted by my actual course design. For each material, please assess whether the complaints about [organization / clarity / rubrics / etc.] are consistent with what the materials show.

Syllabus — [Course Name/Number], [Semester, Year]:

[Paste syllabus text, or summarize key structural elements: learning objectives, assignment schedule, grading criteria, communication policies]

Sample rubric — [Assignment name]:

[Paste rubric]

Sample assignment instructions — [Assignment name]:

[Paste instructions]

Peer observation summary (if available):

[Paste or summarize]

Based on these materials, please assess:

  • Do complaints about “disorganization” hold up against the syllabus structure?
  • Do complaints about “unclear rubrics” hold up against the actual rubrics?
  • Do complaints about “unclear expectations” hold up against the assignment instructions?
  • Do peer observation findings corroborate or contradict the student complaints?

Step 7: Request the Full Analysis Report

Once all materials have been submitted, ask the AI to synthesize everything into a structured report.

Report request prompt template:

Based on all the materials I have shared — my professional record, grade distributions, quantitative scores, written comments, and course materials — please generate a complete bias analysis report structured as follows:

Section 1: Executive Summary

A brief (one paragraph) summary of the overall findings, including the most significant patterns identified and the overall assessment of bias likelihood.

Section 2: Identity-Calibrated Risk Profile

An assessment of which bias types and categories are most applicable given my specific identity position, with an explanation of the compound effects where applicable.

Section 3: Comment-by-Comment Analysis

For each flagged comment, identify the bias category, explain the specific language that triggered the flag, assess whether it is Type A / B / C, and note whether it is corroborated or contradicted by my professional record or course materials.

Section 4: Pattern Analysis

Identify recurring patterns across multiple evaluations or semesters — including the no-win double-bind pattern, the “despite” formulation, absence of positive language, temporal spikes correlated with grade returns, and any coordination signals.

Section 5: Grade Distribution Analysis

Assess the grade-SET credibility matrix for each course — identifying which patterns are consistent with grade-retaliation bias and which might represent genuine pedagogical signals.

Section 6: Credibility Inversion Assessment

Assess specific pedagogical complaints against my documented expertise and experience, identifying which complaints represent a credibility inversion and why.

Section 7: Comparative Differential Notes

Note any areas where the available comparative data (departmental or institutional norms) suggests my evaluations deviate from what would be expected for white male peers in equivalent positions.

Section 8: Recommended Administrative Actions

Based on the analysis, provide recommendations for how administrators should treat this evaluation data — including which comments should be excluded from personnel files, which require independent corroboration before being treated as actionable, and which require immediate escalation.

Section 9: Key Citations

Cite the relevant research literature supporting the findings in this report.

Please write this report in clear, professional language suitable for submission to a tenure committee, department chair, dean, or faculty equity officer.

Step 8: Request Specific Follow-Up Analyses (Optional)

Depending on your situation, you may want to request additional targeted analyses:

Optional follow-up prompts:

For tenure or promotion review:

I am preparing materials for a [tenure / promotion / merit review] decision. Please draft a one-page summary of the bias findings from this analysis that I can submit as a contextualizing statement alongside my SET data. The statement should be written in first person, cite the relevant research literature, and be appropriate for submission to a formal review committee.

For a grievance or complaint:

Based on this analysis, please identify which findings, if any, rise to the level of potential legal concern — including evidence of deliberate defamation, coordinated harassment, or institutional misuse of biased data. Please cite the relevant legal and research frameworks.

For a department chair or dean:

Please draft a brief memo (one page) addressed to a department chair or dean, summarizing the bias findings and recommending specific procedural protections for the review of my SET data. The memo should be professional, evidence-based, and include specific recommended actions.

For temporal pattern analysis:

Please analyze the trajectory of my evaluation scores and comment themes across [X] semesters or years. Specifically, assess whether the pattern of complaints is consistent with accumulating bias rather than accumulating pedagogical deficiency — noting whether complaints increase over time despite my increasing experience, whether they spike after grading events, and whether they remain stable across unrelated courses.

Step 9: Verify, Review, and Document

After receiving the AI analysis:

  1. Read the analysis carefully and note any findings that surprise you or that you think may be inaccurate. AI systems can make errors, and you know your own context better than any AI.

  2. Check the citations. The AI should cite specific research for each finding. If a citation seems unfamiliar or wrong, verify it.

  3. Save the full conversation. The AI’s analysis, including your prompts and the AI’s responses, constitutes a document that may be useful in institutional review processes.

  4. Share with a trusted colleague or advisor before submitting any AI-generated analysis in a formal context. A colleague familiar with the SET bias literature can review the findings for accuracy and appropriateness.

  5. Add your own narrative. The AI analysis is supporting evidence, not a substitute for your own professional voice. Use the findings to inform a personal statement or cover memo that contextualizes the evaluation data in your own words.

Part V: Privacy, Confidentiality, and Ethical Considerations

What to Share with AI

  • Your own evaluation comments and scores: Yes — these are your professional records.

  • De-identified grade distribution data: Yes — with student names and identifiers removed.

  • Your own course materials (syllabi, rubrics, assignments): Yes — these are your professional work products.

  • Peer observation reports about your own teaching: Yes — with observer names included or removed per your judgment.

  • Other faculty members’ evaluation data: No — without explicit consent and appropriate de-identification.

  • Student names in any context: No — always remove before sharing.

  • Any material designated confidential by your institution: Check your institutional policies first.

Platform Considerations

Different AI platforms have different data retention and privacy policies. Before sharing sensitive professional materials:

  • Review the AI platform’s privacy policy and data retention practices.

  • Check whether your institution has an approved AI platform for sensitive professional use.

  • Consider whether using an institutional or enterprise version of an AI tool (which may have stronger data protections) is appropriate for your situation.

What AI Cannot Do

  • AI cannot verify factual claims made in student evaluations.

  • AI cannot access your institution’s database or HR systems.

  • AI cannot make legally binding determinations about bias, discrimination, or harassment.

  • AI analysis does not constitute expert testimony or legal evidence without human expert review and attestation.

  • AI may make errors, miss context, or reflect its own training biases. Human review is essential.

Part VII: Glossary of Key Terms

Behavioral immune system (BIS): The psychological mechanism by which disgust responses, evolved to protect against pathogen threats, are activated by culturally stigmatized groups — including groups coded as disease-carrying (e.g., Haitians following the CDC’s wrongful 1980s AIDS designation). Relevant to Category 13.

Comparative differential standard: The analytical approach of comparing evaluation outcomes for a minoritized instructor against white male peers teaching equivalent courses at equivalent levels of rigor. A differential in outcomes when inputs are equivalent is evidence of bias.

Content substitution: The cognitive phenomenon by which students overestimate the proportion of LGBTQ+ or identity-related content in a course, remembering and evaluating a single identity-related example as a defining pattern. Relevant to Category 12C.

Corroboration gap: The absence of independent peer observation or materials review to verify a student pedagogical complaint. A complaint that cannot be corroborated by independent evidence cannot be treated as an established fact about pedagogy.

Credibility inversion: The paradox in which negative pedagogical feedback is treated as an actionable signal even when it directly contradicts the instructor’s documented expertise, years of experience, and scholarly record.

DFW rate: The percentage of students in a course who receive a D grade, an F grade, or who withdraw. Frequently misused as a proxy for teaching effectiveness; actually confounded by student preparation, course type, and grading standards.

Double double-bind: The pattern in which an instructor is penalized at both extremes of every evaluative spectrum simultaneously — too strict and too lenient, too much work and not enough challenge. A no-win pattern across multiple dimensions is diagnostic of identity bias.

Disgusted-organized bias: Bias activated by the emotion of disgust rather than contempt or anger, associated with groups historically coded as disease-carrying or morally contaminating. Distinct from general racial bias in its psychological mechanism and evaluative expression.

Follow-on course performance: Student performance in courses taken after a prerequisite or gateway course. Research consistently shows this is the gold standard for measuring teaching effectiveness, and is negatively correlated with SET scores in high-credibility studies.

“Forever foreigner” trope: The pattern of marking a native-born, native-English-speaking faculty member as permanently outside the national or institutional community based on ethnic identity. Language fluency provides no protection when ethnic stigma is the operative bias.

Grade-retaliation bias: The pattern of lower SET scores in courses where grades are lower, driven by student dissatisfaction with grades rather than genuine pedagogical assessment.

Novelty attachment: The cognitive phenomenon by which students notice and register LGBTQ+-inclusive or identity-related course examples more prominently than equivalent heteronormative examples, leading to overestimation of their frequency.

Presumption of incompetence: The documented tendency of students to assume lower competence, authority, and expertise in faculty of color before any instruction occurs, regardless of actual qualifications.

Proxy bias: Bias expressed through ostensibly neutral pedagogical complaints — about grading, organization, clarity, or workload — that research establishes are systematically more negative for minoritized faculty even when the actual pedagogy is equivalent to that of white male peers.

Racial taxation: Lazos (2012)‘s term for the phenomenon in which the burden of students’ discomfort with equity-related course content is placed on the instructor rather than the content, expressed through evaluative accusations of bias and agenda-pushing.

Success inversion (“Despite” formulation): The pattern in which students acknowledge their own learning gains while simultaneously denying the instructor’s contribution, attributing success to themselves and describing it as occurring despite poor teaching.

Type 1–4 bias: The four-category classification system used in this guide: Type 1 (Direct Identity Bias), Type 2 (Proxy Bias), Type 3 (Stigmatized Identity Bias), Type 4 (Deliberate Defamation).
