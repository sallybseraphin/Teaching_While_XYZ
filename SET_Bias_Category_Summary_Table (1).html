1

Personality & Temperament

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Affective/temperamental language replaces intellectual or pedagogical language. Professional assertiveness is systematically misread as hostility, regardless of actual conduct.

Example Phrases

  • Has a chip on their shoulder
  • Seems angry all the time
  • Went on a rant
  • Very off-putting
  • Blew up at students
  • Overreacted

Key Sources

Harlow (2003); Tuitt et al. (2009); Motro et al. (2022); Smith & Hawkins (2011)

2

Authority & Competence Challenges

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Presumption of incompetence: credentials and expertise denied regardless of qualifications. Pre-course bias shapes assessments before any instruction occurs.

Example Phrases

  • Doesn't know the material
  • Not qualified to teach this
  • Students knew more than the professor
  • Not a real expert
  • How did they get this job

Key Sources

Gutiérrez y Muhs et al. (2012); Chávez & Mitchell (2020); Anderson & Smith (2005); Reid (2010)

3

Accent & Language Bias

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Non-native accent conflated with incompetence; comprehension rated lower regardless of actual clarity. Can operate independently of English fluency when ethnic identity is stigmatized.

Example Phrases

  • Hard to understand because of their accent
  • Needs to work on their English
  • The accent made it impossible to learn
  • Should not be teaching if they can't speak clearly

Key Sources

Subtirelu (2015); Rubin & Smith (1990); Chávez & Mitchell (2020); Heffernan (2022)

4

Relatability & Fit

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

"Forever foreigner" trope marks instructor as not belonging based on identity, not conduct. Racialized expectations shape interpersonal assessments before any interaction occurs.

Example Phrases

  • Doesn't fit in here
  • Not the type of professor I expected
  • Feels like they're from a different world
  • Seems out of place

Key Sources

Reid (2010); Pittman (2010); Anderson & Smith (2005); Chávez & Mitchell (2020)

5

Gendered Language Patterns

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Women evaluated on emotional affect and appearance rather than intellect. Held to contradictory double standards of warmth and authority that do not apply to male peers.

Example Phrases

  • Bossy
  • Should smile more
  • Let students walk all over her
  • Too aggressive for a woman
  • Came across as weak
  • Tries too hard to be liked

Key Sources

Schmidt (2015); MacNell et al. (2014); Boring et al. (2016); Sprague & Massoni (2005); Mengel et al. (2019)

6

Racialized Language Patterns

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Black faculty receive the lowest SET scores of any racial group. Explicit racial coding appears in open-ended comments; authority is challenged in ways absent from evaluations of white peers.

Example Phrases

  • Too loud
  • Makes everything about race
  • Biased against white students
  • Plays favorites
  • Not the typical professor
  • I felt singled out

Key Sources

Smith & Hawkins (2011); Pittman (2010); Subtirelu (2015); Harris-Perry (2011); Harlow (2003)

7

Bias Claims Turned Outward

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Students attribute their own discomfort to instructor bias, accusing the instructor of the very prejudice being enacted. Lazos (2012) terms this pattern "racial taxation."

Example Phrases

  • Has a personal agenda
  • Too political
  • Silences students
  • Grades based on whether you agree with them
  • Creates a hostile environment for [majority group] students

Key Sources

Lazos (2012); Pittman (2010); Harlow (2003); Gutiérrez y Muhs et al. (2012)

8

Course Content as Proxy Bias

Compound (Multiple Types)

Mechanism

Complaints about subject matter function as attacks on the instructor's identity and right to teach. Evaluations of equity-related courses are significantly more negative regardless of instructional quality.

Example Phrases

  • This class is too political
  • Felt like propaganda
  • Turned every topic into a race issue
  • Pushed a liberal agenda
  • The class was more about their identity than the content

Key Sources

Gutiérrez y Muhs et al. (2012); Lazos (2012); Pittman (2010); ScienceDirect (2022)

9A

Grading & Assessment Complaints

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Grade satisfaction — not instructional quality — drives SET scores (r = .45). Women and faculty of color are penalized more severely for identical grading decisions than white male peers.

Example Phrases

  • Grading is unfair
  • Rubrics are unclear
  • Exams don't reflect what we studied
  • Inconsistent grading
  • You can't win no matter what you do

Key Sources

Kogan et al. (2022); Buser et al. (2022); Boring et al. (2016); St. Olaf IEA (2025)

9B

Feedback Timing & Responsiveness

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Bias affects even putatively objective measures such as turnaround time. Women and faculty of color are held to higher emotional labor and availability expectations than white male peers.

Example Phrases

  • Takes forever to return assignments
  • Never responds to emails
  • Doesn't hold enough office hours
  • Doesn't care about student success
  • Unapproachable when you need help

Key Sources

Boring et al. (2016); Inside Higher Ed (2019); Sprague & Massoni (2005); Hochschild (1983)

9C

Workload & Rigor Complaints

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Academic rigor is penalized in a consumer satisfaction model. Minoritized faculty are less able to reduce rigor without compounding authority challenges; rigorous instructors receive worse ratings yet produce better learning outcomes.

Example Phrases

  • Way too much work
  • Sets students up to fail
  • Other professors don't assign this much
  • Standards are unreasonably high
  • Not worth the effort required

Key Sources

Kogan et al. (2022); Marsh & Roche (2000); St. Olaf IEA (2025); Chronicle (2025)

9D

Syllabus, Structure & Organization

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

The same syllabus is perceived as less organized when attributed to a woman or faculty of color. Women face greater scrutiny of organizational self-presentation, independent of actual course design quality.

Example Phrases

  • Disorganized
  • The syllabus is confusing
  • Slides are not posted on time
  • I never know what's due
  • No clear learning objectives

Key Sources

Boring et al. (2016); Schmidt (2015); Sprague & Massoni (2005); UNC Charlotte ADVANCE (2023)

9E

Class Delivery & Instructional Clarity

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Male instructors are perceived as clearer and more enthusiastic regardless of actual instructional quality. Identical delivery is rated differently depending on the instructor's perceived identity.

Example Phrases

  • Doesn't explain things clearly
  • Lectures aren't useful
  • Loses control of the class
  • Class feels unplanned
  • Doesn't command respect

Key Sources

St. Olaf IEA (2025); UNC Charlotte ADVANCE (2023); MacNell et al. (2014); Boring et al. (2016)

10A

The Rigor Penalty

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Rigorous courses produce lower SET scores. This penalty falls disproportionately on minoritized faculty. The most effective teachers for long-term learning receive the worst contemporaneous evaluations.

Example Phrases

  • Too hard for no reason
  • Standards are impossibly high
  • Doesn't curve even when everyone fails
  • Penalizes students for not being perfect
  • Sets students up to fail

Key Sources

Carrell & West (2010); Braga et al. (2014); Uttl et al. (2017); Minding the Campus (2025)

10B

The Success Inversion

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Students attribute learning gains to themselves while denying instructor credit — the "Despite" formulation. Self-serving attribution bias is amplified by the presumption of incompetence for minoritized faculty.

Example Phrases

  • I learned despite the poor instruction
  • Had to teach myself
  • Study groups saved me in this course
  • Got through it no thanks to...
  • The book was great, the professor not so much

Key Sources

Gutiérrez y Muhs et al. (2012); Uttl et al. (2017); Carrell & West (2010); Weiner (1985)

10C

The Double Double-Bind

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

No behavioral choice is correct. The instructor is penalized at both extremes of every spectrum simultaneously. A no-win pattern appearing across multiple dimensions is diagnostic of identity bias.

Example Phrases

  • "Too strict" AND "too lenient" simultaneously
  • "Too much work" AND "doesn't challenge students"
  • "Too cold" AND "too emotional"
  • "Too formal" AND "too casual"

Key Sources

Sprague & Massoni (2005); Boring et al. (2016); MacNell et al. (2014); Buser et al. (2022)

11

Structural & Tonal Tropes

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Six named tropes with distinct mechanisms: Tone Policing; Angry Black Woman/Sapphire; Model Minority Inversion; Authority Challenge; Double Bind; Emotional Labor Expectations; Guilt Narrative.

Example Phrases

  • Went on a rant [Sapphire trope]
  • Not as deferential as I expected [Model Minority Inversion]
  • Made me feel bad for being white [Guilt Narrative]
  • Let students walk all over her [Double Bind]

Key Sources

Harris-Perry (2011); Motro et al. (2022); Sprague & Massoni (2005); Hochschild (1983); West (2008)

12A

LGBTQ+ — 'Gay Agenda' Trope

Compound (Multiple Types)

Mechanism

Students perceive LG faculty as more politically biased even when using identical syllabi. LGBTQ+ identity is coded as inherently political; disclosure alone doubles the volume of critical evaluation remarks.

Example Phrases

  • Has a political agenda
  • The class felt like an LGBTQ advocacy session
  • Too focused on gender/sexuality issues
  • Kept bringing up irrelevant personal information

Key Sources

Anderson & Kanner (2011); Ripley et al. (2012); CBE Life Sciences (2024)

12B

LGBTQ+ — Credibility Challenges

Type 3 — Stigmatized Identity

Mechanism

Identity disclosure alone produces doubling of critical remarks. Instructors are perceived as less credible and effective after coming out, even when course content and quality are unchanged.

Example Phrases

  • Not qualified to teach this topic objectively
  • Seems to only care about LGBTQ issues
  • [Lower competence ratings following disclosure with no pedagogical change]

Key Sources

Russ et al. (2002); Anderson & Kanner (2011); Frontiers in Education (2024)

12C

LGBTQ+ — Content Distortion

Type 3 — Stigmatized Identity

Mechanism

Novelty attachment and content substitution: students overestimate LGBTQ+ content proportion. A single identity-related example is remembered and evaluated as a defining pattern of the entire course.

Example Phrases

  • Spent too much time on LGBTQ examples
  • Everything was about gay/transgender people
  • The course wasn't what I expected [after a single identity-related reference]

Key Sources

Ripley et al. (2012); Anderson & Kanner (2011)

12D

LGBTQ+ — Disclosure Retaliation

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

Deliberate negative evaluations submitted after identity disclosure; documented as intentional retaliatory conduct; spike in negative scores with no corresponding change in course design or delivery.

Example Phrases

  • [Spike in negative scores immediately post-disclosure]
  • [Deliberate use of wrong pronouns in evaluation text]
  • Made me feel unwelcome for not agreeing with the LGBTQ agenda

Key Sources

Frontiers in Education (2024); Russ et al. (2002); De Souza (2018)

12E

LGBTQ+ — Discomfort as Harm

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Students frame the instructor's authentic identity as a hostile act. The instructor's existence is coded as a threat to the classroom safety of majority students.

Example Phrases

  • I didn't feel comfortable in this class
  • The professor made me feel judged for being straight/Christian
  • The classroom wasn't a safe space for my views

Key Sources

CBE Life Sciences (2024); Boston University SPH (2024)

12F

LGBTQ+ — Transgender/Nonbinary

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

Deliberate misgendering constitutes targeted harassment. Gender identity is framed as a professional distraction or violation. Nearly half of nonbinary workers report monthly workplace discrimination.

Example Phrases

  • [Deliberate wrong pronouns or name used in evaluation]
  • Made class about their gender stuff
  • Unprofessional to bring up personal gender issues in class

Key Sources

Kreitzer & Sweet-Cushman (2021); Inside Higher Ed (2023); CBE Life Sciences (2024)

12G

LGBTQ+ — Intersectional Compounding

Compound (Multiple Types)

Mechanism

Gay faculty of color face homophobic evaluations compounded by racial bias. The compound effect is multiplicative, not additive. Professional exclusion is documented alongside evaluative hostility.

Example Phrases

  • [Homophobic language combined with racialized authority challenges in the same evaluation]
  • [Professional opinions dismissed alongside explicit evaluative hostility]

Key Sources

Misawa (2015); Frontiers in Education (2024); Anderson & Kanner (2011)

13A

Stigmatized Identity — Disgust Mechanism

Type 3 — Stigmatized Identity

Mechanism

Groups historically coded as disease-carrying (e.g., Haitians, following the CDC's wrongful 1980s AIDS designation) activate disgust via the behavioral immune system. This operates in native English speakers with no detectable accent.

Example Phrases

  • [Language suggesting physical aversion or avoidance without pedagogical cause]
  • [Classroom described as uncomfortable with no specific incident cited]
  • [Disproportionate emotional intensity relative to the stated complaint]

Key Sources

Pascal et al. (2023); Kusche & Barker (2019); Hodson et al. (2013); UUSC (2025)

13B

Stigmatized Identity — Disgust Language

Type 3 — Stigmatized Identity

Mechanism

Disgust-organized language is displaced onto neutral-appearing pedagogical complaints. The evaluator processes the instructor as an aversive stimulus independent of any conduct or course quality.

Example Phrases

  • [Language implying contamination, dirtiness, or moral taint]
  • [Complaints with emotional intensity disproportionate to their stated object]
  • [Expressed desire to minimize contact with the instructor]

Key Sources

Pascal et al. (2023); Hodson et al. (2013); Kusche & Barker (2019)

13C

Stigmatized Identity — 'Forever Foreigner'

Type 3 — Stigmatized Identity

Mechanism

Native-born, native-English-speaking faculty are marked as permanently outside the national or institutional community based on ethnic identity. Language fluency provides no protection when ethnic stigma is the operative bias.

Example Phrases

  • Doesn't fit in here
  • Not what I expected
  • Surprising for someone in this position
  • [Any reference to national origin in an evaluative context]

Key Sources

Vox (2024); IBW21 (2024); Fan et al. (2019)

13D

Stigmatized Identity — Political Amplification

Type 3 — Stigmatized Identity

Mechanism

Nationally amplified ethnic rhetoric (e.g., 2024 anti-Haitian campaign rhetoric) activates dormant student biases. Evaluation timing relative to political events is a critical contextual variable for AI analysis.

Example Phrases

  • [Anti-Haitian political rhetoric surfacing in evaluation framing or language]
  • [Temporal correlation between politically amplified rhetoric and evaluation score spikes]

Key Sources

Harvard Law Review (2024); The Intercept (2024); Common Dreams (2024)

13E

Stigmatized Identity — Cultural Content

Compound (Multiple Types)

Mechanism

Course content related to the instructor's stigmatized national identity is dismissed as illegitimate scholarship or political indoctrination, attacking the instructor's professional right to teach their own field.

Example Phrases

  • Too focused on [country or region]
  • That history isn't relevant to us
  • Why are we learning about [place] in this class?
  • [Cultural practices framed as exotic or irrational]

Key Sources

Lazos (2012); Gutiérrez y Muhs et al. (2012); Fan et al. (2019)

13F

Stigmatized Identity — Intersectional Compounding

Compound (Multiple Types)

Mechanism

Race and national stigma operate simultaneously and multiplicatively. Haitian faculty face anti-Black racism AND anti-Haitian ethnic stigma. The compound effect is greater than either alone and is currently unmeasured in the literature.

Example Phrases

  • [Anti-Black language combined with ethnicity-specific disgust-organized language in the same evaluation]
  • [Racialized authority challenges combined with 'forever foreigner' framing]

Key Sources

The Conversation (2024); Pascal et al. (2023); Crenshaw (1989)

14A

Defamation — Anonymity Enablement

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

89% of Americans report that anonymity enables cruelty. Anonymous SET platforms provide a consequence-free environment for false claims; SETs are deliberately weaponized as bullying instruments against untenured and contingent faculty.

Example Phrases

  • [Structural condition: no accountability mechanism, no named complainant, no right to respond before career harm occurs]

Key Sources

Burke & Spurgeon (2020); Rodriguez/AAUP (2019); Heffernan (2023)

14B

Defamation — Contrapower Harassment (ACPH)

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

45% of faculty report hostile evaluations; 25% have received threatening communications. Women faculty face more severe psychological outcomes including anxiety, stress-related illness, and intent to quit.

Example Phrases

  • [Sexually explicit comments about the instructor]
  • [Explicit threats or intimidating language]
  • I HATE YOU / I HATE EVERYTHING ABOUT YOU
  • [Personal attacks on family, appearance, or off-campus life]

Key Sources

Lampman et al. (2009, 2012); Burke & Spurgeon (2020); Inside Higher Ed (2018)

14C

Defamation — Misconduct Accusations

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

False or unadjudicated misconduct claims enter personnel files without investigation. Faculty have no opportunity to respond before career harm occurs; claims are treated as equivalent to verified complaints.

Example Phrases

  • [Accusations of discrimination stated as established fact]
  • [Sexual harassment accusations without any investigation or due process]
  • [Policy violation or plagiarism claims without supporting evidence]

Key Sources

Deo (2015); NACUA (n.d.); Nesenoff & Miltenberg LLP (2020)

14D

Defamation — False Factual Claims

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

Fabricated accounts of in-class incidents; false claims about assignment explanations, attendance, or grading that are provably false but treated as valid pedagogical feedback in personnel review without verification.

Example Phrases

  • [Claims assignments were never explained when LMS records show they were]
  • [Claims instructor was absent when attendance records contradict this]
  • [Fabricated in-class incidents not corroborated by any other student]

Key Sources

Deo (2015); NACUA (n.d.); ATIXA (2021)

14E

Defamation — Coordinated Attacks

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

Harassment is coordinated on anonymous forums (4chan, Reddit) with strategies shared openly. Coordination signals are detectable through linguistic pattern analysis of multiple evaluations from the same term.

Example Phrases

  • [Multiple evaluations using identical or near-identical phrasing]
  • [Sudden spike in negative evaluations after a specific triggering event]
  • [Evaluations referencing social media posts or outside-classroom content]

Key Sources

Kamenetz/NPR (2018); McKay et al. (2021); Rodriguez/AAUP (2019)

14F

Defamation — Grade Retaliation

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

Deliberately negative evaluations submitted as retribution for grading decisions; instrumentally motivated rather than genuinely pedagogical. Detectable by submission timing, explicit grade references, and contrast with prior evaluations.

Example Phrases

  • [Evaluations submitted immediately after grade disputes]
  • [Explicit references to specific grades received in evaluation text]
  • [Sudden shift from previously positive evaluations correlated with a specific grading event]

Key Sources

Nesenoff & Miltenberg LLP (2020); Burke & Spurgeon (2020); Kogan et al. (2022)

14G

Defamation — Institutional Weaponization

Type 4 — Deliberate Defamation

Mechanism

SET data is deliberately wielded by administrators and departments to disadvantage minoritized faculty. Biased student data is laundered as apparently objective evidence for tenure denial, non-renewal, and salary decisions.

Example Phrases

  • [Tenure denial citing SET data while equivalent data for white male peers is ignored]
  • [Administrators citing instruments they acknowledge as flawed to justify adverse personnel actions]

Key Sources

LawProfBlawg/Above the Law (2018); Rodriguez/AAUP (2019); Deo (2019)

15

Absence of Positive Language

Type 1 — Direct Identity Bias

Mechanism

Positive intellectual descriptors (brilliant, genius, inspiring) appear significantly less often in evaluations of minoritized faculty even when performance is equivalent or superior. Their systematic absence is a measurable comparative bias signal.

Example Phrases

  • [Absent: brilliant, genius, inspiring, natural teacher, commands respect]
  • [Absent: clear, articulate, authoritative, best professor I've had]
  • [Detectable only through comparison against white male peers teaching equivalent courses]

Key Sources

Schmidt (2015); MacNell et al. (2014); Boring et al. (2016); Chávez & Mitchell (2020)

16A

Credibility Inversion — Expertise Paradox

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Published SoTL expertise directly contradicts the plausibility of student complaints in the same domain. Peer-reviewed scholarship constitutes higher-quality evidence of pedagogical competence than anonymous student ratings.

Example Phrases

  • 'Disorganized' complaints against a faculty member with publications on course design
  • 'Unclear rubrics' complaints against a published assessment design scholar
  • 'Hard to follow' against a faculty member with 20+ years of teaching and documented positive outcomes

Key Sources

Gutiérrez y Muhs et al. (2012); Inside Higher Ed (2022); Boring et al. (2016)

16B

Credibility Inversion — Corroboration Gap

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

A pedagogical complaint cannot be treated as actionable without independent peer observation or materials review. Absence of corroboration is itself a finding — it cannot be resolved by accumulating more SET data.

Example Phrases

  • [Any persistent complaint uncorroborated by peer observation, teaching portfolio, or LMS records]
  • [Complaints contradicted by syllabi, marked work samples, or exam blueprints in the teaching file]

Key Sources

Esarey & Valdes (2020); UNC Charlotte ADVANCE (2023); St. Olaf IEA (2025)

16C

Credibility Inversion — Temporal Stability

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Complaints increasing despite accumulating teaching experience; spikes correlated with grade returns; identical complaints stable across unrelated courses. All are bias-consistent temporal patterns, not patterns of genuine pedagogical deficiency.

Example Phrases

  • [Same complaint categories appearing in Year 1 and Year 20 of teaching]
  • [Complaint intensity spiking after midterm grades are returned]
  • [Identical complaints across courses with entirely different content and student populations]

Key Sources

Inside Higher Ed (2022); Boring et al. (2016); Buser et al. (2022); Pascal et al. (2023)

16D

Credibility Inversion — Signal Classification

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Three-type signal classification with identity-calibrated thresholds: Type A (genuine, corroborated), Type B (probable bias artifact), Type C (harassment or defamation requiring immediate escalation).

Example Phrases

  • Type A: corroborated by peer observation + specific + not contradicting documented expertise
  • Type B: uncorroborated + contradicts expertise + consistent with bias literature for this identity position
  • Type C: false factual claims + coordinated pattern + explicit harassment language

Key Sources

Deo (2015); Rodriguez/AAUP (2019); Lampman et al. (2009); NACUA (n.d.)

16E

Credibility Inversion — Comparative Differential

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Bias is most detectable when identical pedagogy generates positive comments for white male peers and negative comments for minoritized faculty. The differential itself is the evidence of bias, not any absolute level of complaints.

Example Phrases

  • 'Disorganized' for a Black woman instructor; 'clear and well-structured' for a white male colleague teaching an equivalent course at equivalent rigor
  • [Same grading policy produces complaints for one instructor and not the other]

Key Sources

Chávez & Mitchell (2020); Smith & Hawkins (2011); Boring et al. (2016); MacNell et al. (2014)

17A

Grade Distributions — Grade-SET Relationship

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Grade satisfaction (r = .45 with SET scores) drives evaluations. A grading reputation effect penalizes rigorous instructors in subsequent courses. Follow-on course performance is negatively correlated with SET scores in high-credibility studies.

Example Phrases

  • [SET scores positively correlated with concurrent grades but negatively correlated with subsequent course performance]
  • [Better teaching for long-term learning produces worse contemporaneous satisfaction ratings]

Key Sources

Carrell & West (2010); Braga et al. (2014); Stroebe (2020); Kogan et al. (2022)

17B–C

Grade Distributions — Credibility Matrix

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Four-framework grade-SET interpretation matrix: (1) Low grades + negative SETs = probable grade retaliation. (2) High grades + negative SETs = probable identity bias independent of grading. Both require comparison against white male peers.

Example Phrases

  • Low mean GPA + negative SETs → probable grade-retaliation bias artifact requiring independent corroboration
  • High mean GPA + negative SETs → probable identity bias — grade dissatisfaction cannot explain this pattern

Key Sources

Stroebe (2020); Evans & Carrell (2011); Kogan et al. (2022); UNC Charlotte ADVANCE (2023)

17D

Grade Distributions — DFW Rate Misuse

Compound (Multiple Types)

Mechanism

DFW rates are confounded by student preparation, course type, and grading standards. They have been used at HBCUs (Savannah State case) to penalize Black faculty maintaining high academic standards — with no peer-reviewed justification.

Example Phrases

  • [Unwritten policy treating DFW rate >25% as prima facie evidence of poor teaching]
  • [DFW-rate policy that forces grade inflation to avoid institutional sanctions — disproportionately affecting minoritized faculty]

Key Sources

Inside Higher Ed (2018); Uttl et al. (2017); Stroebe (2020); SIUE Grade Analytics (n.d.)

17E

Grade Distributions — Follow-On Performance

Type 2 — Proxy Bias

Mechanism

Student performance in subsequent courses is the gold standard for teaching effectiveness — superior to SET scores, concurrent grades, and DFW rates. Academic rank and experience predict follow-on performance positively but SET scores negatively.

Example Phrases

  • [Students from rigorous instructor's prerequisite sections outperform in follow-on courses despite that instructor's lower SET scores]
  • [Random-assignment Air Force Academy data confirms negative correlation between SET scores and subsequent course performance]

Key Sources

Carrell & West (2010); Braga et al. (2014); Kornell & Hausman (2016); Preprints.org (2025)

17F

Grade Distributions — Grade Inflation Trap

Compound (Multiple Types)

Mechanism

38% of professors have made courses easier in response to SET pressure. This pressure is asymmetric and more severe for minoritized faculty. Grade inflation harms students' subsequent course performance and professional preparation.

Example Phrases

  • [Instructor reduces rigor → grades rise → SET scores improve → follow-on course performance declines]
  • [DFW-policy trap: maintain standards = sanctions OR inflate grades = credibility critique — neither is safe for minoritized faculty]

Key Sources

Preprints.org (2025); AAUP (2018); Stroebe (2020); Inside Higher Ed (2018)
